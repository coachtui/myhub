Vistra is the inverse of every "priced for perfection" AI name. Its core cash engine — Ongoing Operations Adjusted EBITDA — jumped 31% year over year last quarter, it reaffirmed full-year guidance, and it has bought back roughly 30% of its shares. And the stock? Down about 38% from its high and sitting right on its 52-week low. A year ago Vistra was a market darling riding the "AI needs power and nuclear is scarce" narrative to a parabolic run. That trade has now fully unwound. The question worth answering: is the fleet's cash generation being ignored because of a fixable regulatory cloud, or is the downtrend telling you the AI-power premium was never real?

The Business

Vistra (NYSE: VST) is an integrated power company — it both generates electricity and sells it to retail customers, which smooths out the wild swings a pure merchant generator would face. Two pieces:

  • Generation fleet. A big, dispatchable mix — natural gas, nuclear, coal, plus growing solar and battery storage — spread across Texas (ERCOT) and the East (PJM). The nuclear is the crown jewel: Comanche Peak in Texas, plus the PJM plants it picked up buying Energy Harbor. Nuclear is exactly the 24/7 carbon-free baseload hyperscalers want to power data centers.
  • Retail. One of the largest competitive retail electricity businesses in the country, from California to Maine. This is the hedge — when it owns both the power plant and the customer, it's less exposed to raw commodity swings than a merchant generator.

The thesis in one line: US electricity demand is inflecting for the first time in decades — data centers, electrification, and reindustrialization all pulling at once — and the companies that own dispatchable generation (especially nuclear) are the landlords of that scarcity. Vistra is one of the biggest.

What Q2 Actually Said

Reported August 7 (quarter ended June 30). The disconnect between the business and the stock is the whole story:

  • Ongoing Operations Adjusted EBITDA: up 31% YoY — this is the real cash-generating metric, and it surged
  • By region: Texas EBITDA up 119% to $311M; East up ~53% — broad-based strength, and notably strongest in ERCOT where power demand is booming
  • GAAP net income: took a hit from a ~$488M year-over-year swing in unrealized mark-to-market derivative (hedging) losses. That's non-cash accounting noise, not a deterioration in the business — but it's why the GAAP headline looked ugly
  • Capital return: aggressive — Vistra has retired roughly 30% of its shares through buybacks, with more authorization to deploy through 2027, plus a (modestly raised) $0.23 quarterly dividend. Fewer shares means every dollar of that growing EBITDA is spread over a smaller base
  • Guidance: reaffirmed full-year 2026 (~$6B+ Ongoing EBITDA). Analysts model roughly $9 in EPS this year and ~$11 next

So: cash earnings up double digits, guidance intact, shares shrinking fast. On the fundamentals, this was a strong quarter. The stock did not care.

Why It Got Cut By a Third

Three forces, in order of how much they matter.

The PJM regulatory overhang. This is the specific thing spooking the whole IPP group. Much of the AI-power thesis rested on "co-location" and behind-the-meter deals — plugging a data center straight into a power plant. Regulators in PJM (via the amended interconnection-service-agreement fight at FERC) have raised hard questions about whether and how those deals are allowed. That uncertainty froze a chunk of the deal flow the market had already priced in. Notably, management pointed out that ERCOT — Texas, where Comanche Peak sits — doesn't fall under that PJM jurisdiction, which is a real advantage. But the cloud is over the sector.

The AI-power trade unwound. VST, Constellation, Talen, GE Vernova and the rest went vertical in 2024–25 on the nuclear/AI narrative. When momentum money rotated out, the names that ran the most gave back the most — and VST round-tripped a huge move regardless of the quarterly numbers.

The "is this multiple even real" question. Under the AI story, the market paid a growth multiple for what is, structurally, a capital-intensive, commodity-exposed power business. The de-rate is partly the market re-asking whether an IPP deserves a premium at all. Tellingly, management itself said the business's fundamentals "don't depend on doing a lot of data center deals" — reassuring if you believe Texas demand carries it, but also a quiet admission that the data-center deal flow is slower than the hype implied.

The Thesis

Bull case: you're buying a structural winner in dispatchable power — gas plus scarce nuclear — at ~15x this year's earnings and ~12x next year's, down 38% from the high, while the company grows cash EBITDA 31% and retires 30% of its own shares. The load-growth story (data centers + electrification + reindustrialization) is real and multi-year, ERCOT sidesteps the PJM mess, and the buyback is a powerful per-share tailwind that works even if the stock stays cheap. If the power-demand thesis is even half right, this is a beaten-down compounder.

Bear case: it's still a capital-intensive merchant-power business whose earnings swing with power and gas prices, dressed in an AI costume the market is now removing. The PJM regulatory question is unresolved and could keep a lid on the co-location deals the premium assumed. GAAP earnings are noisy, the chart is in a clear downtrend at the lows, and "cheap" can stay cheap — or get cheaper — if power prices soften or the data-center buildout digests.

What would change my mind: a clean resolution of the PJM/FERC co-location rules (either direction — clarity itself would help), a signed marquee data-center power deal (especially at Comanche Peak), or the stock printing a higher low above its 52-week low with RSI turning up. On the downside, a decisive break of the $132.66 low, or a cut to that reaffirmed guidance, would say the bear case is winning.
