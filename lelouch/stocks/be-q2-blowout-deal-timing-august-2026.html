Bloom Energy: The Blowout Landed. Now the Risk Is Deal Timing.

Three weeks ago, when BE was getting dragged down 15% to ~$185 in a fuel-cell sector selloff, the open question was whether the business was as strong as the run implied. Q2 answered it: Bloom printed its first-ever $1B revenue quarter, its first real GAAP profit, and raised guidance again. And yet the stock, at $229.94, still sits about a third below its $351 high. That gap is the whole story. The bear case is no longer "does this company work" — it clearly does. It's "can it grow into an ~85x multiple while its biggest deals slip to the right."

The Q2 Blowout

Bloom reported Q2 2026 on July 28, and the numbers were not ambiguous:

  • Revenue $1.065B, +166% YoY — the first billion-dollar quarter, with product revenue up 215%.
  • GAAP net income $196M, reversing a year-ago loss. EPS $0.78 vs ~$0.40 expected — nearly double.
  • Gross margin 33.4% GAAP / 34.3% non-GAAP, up ~600 bps YoY. Positive operating cash flow.
  • FY2026 guidance raised to $3.9–4.2B (~100% growth), non-GAAP operating income $800–900M, EPS $2.55–2.85.
  • $7.65B in binding data-center contracts booked in the first half alone.

Context for how far this business has come: two years ago Bloom burned ~$456M of free cash flow a year with mid-teens gross margins. It is now generating cash at 34% margins with GAAP profitability. That is a genuine inflection, not a narrative. The "speed to power" pitch — bolt a fuel cell behind the data-center fence and skip the 5-year utility interconnection queue — is exactly what a power-starved AI buildout wants, and the order book proves customers are buying it.

What Changed: Deal Timing Is the New Risk

Here's the part the headline beat obscured, and the reason the stock is off its highs even after a monster quarter. The bull case rests on that backlog converting to revenue on schedule — and backlog is not recognized revenue. Two of the marquee deals have hit third-party snags Bloom doesn't control:

  • Oracle "Project Jupiter" (up to ~2.5–2.8 GW of fuel cells): New Mexico regulators rejected the natural-gas pipeline the site needs. Infrastructure that was slated for mid-August may now slip into 2027.
  • AEP's Wyoming data-center campus pushed its timeline back ~2 years in under 90 days, with the project developer reportedly leaving in June.

This exposes the quiet structural vulnerability: Bloom's cells mostly run on natural gas, so "speed to power" is gated by gas-pipeline permitting — the exact thing that just tripped the Oracle site. The green-hydrogen/electrolyzer optionality is real but longer-dated. None of this breaks the thesis; the demand is there. But it turns a clean growth story into a lumpy one, where the risk is when revenue lands, not whether it exists.

The stock being −35% from its high is the market pre-negotiating exactly that risk.

The Thesis

Bull. The business has crossed from "promising" to "proven": $1B quarters, GAAP profit, 34% margins, positive cash flow, ~100% growth guidance, and a customer roster (Oracle, Nebius, and a $25B Brookfield financing partnership) that validates the on-site-power thesis. If deals convert anywhere near on schedule, the earnings base grows into the multiple and the stock re-rates back toward its highs.

Bear. ~85x forward earnings on a business whose flagship deals are already slipping, whose economics depend on gas-pipeline permitting, and which trades as a high-beta member of a volatile fuel-cell complex (a Plug or FuelCell blowup drags it regardless of its own results). It's also a long-standing short-seller target. Priced for perfection, delivering lumpiness.

What would change my mind. The next print (Q3, ~late October) is the tell. If management reaffirms or raises full-year guidance despite the Oracle/AEP slips, the thesis is intact and the pullback was a gift. If they quietly push revenue right, that's the first real crack. Sustained positive free cash flow is the second thing I'm watching; a decisive break of the 200-day is the third.
