What Oklo Actually Does (or Will Do)

Oklo is building small modular reactors — specifically a sodium-cooled fast reactor called the Aurora Powerhouse. Unlike traditional nuclear plants, which are single 1,000+ megawatt installations that take 15-20 years to permit and build, Oklo's Aurora design is modular: 15-50 MW units intended to be factory-fabricated and deployed at customer sites in months, not decades. The business model isn't selling reactors — it's selling power. Oklo plans to own and operate the reactors and sell electricity to customers under long-term power purchase agreements (PPAs), similar to how a solar developer finances, builds, and sells power from a solar farm.

The Aurora's fuel is HALEU — high-assay low-enriched uranium — which Oklo can produce in part by recycling spent nuclear fuel from existing U.S. reactors. The sodium cooling system runs at atmospheric pressure (unlike water-cooled reactors that require high-pressure containment), which Oklo argues makes the design inherently safer and simpler. The company was founded in 2013, went public via SPAC in 2024, and has never generated commercial revenue. No Aurora Powerhouse is currently operational.

Why AI Created This Opportunity

The AI buildout has a power problem. A standard server rack draws 5-10 kilowatts. An AI GPU rack running NVIDIA H100s draws 60-100 kilowatts — 10-20x more power density. As hyperscalers build out AI training and inference infrastructure at unprecedented scale, their power consumption is growing faster than the grid can absorb. Microsoft has committed to carbon neutrality. Google has pledged 24/7 carbon-free energy. Meta is targeting 100% renewable power. And every one of these companies is discovering that solar and wind don't solve the "always-on" problem — AI inference runs 24 hours a day, and the sun doesn't always shine.

Nuclear is the only zero-carbon energy source that provides baseload power — constant, 24/7 generation regardless of weather. The problem with conventional nuclear is the timeline and cost: $10-30 billion per plant, 15-20 years to build, decades of permitting. Small modular reactors promise to solve this with factory production and shorter timelines. If they work, they are the answer to AI's power problem. That "if they work" is what the entire Oklo valuation debate is about.

The Regulatory Hurdle: NRC License

This is the single biggest risk in the entire thesis. In 2022, the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission denied Oklo's first combined license application for its Aurora reactor — not because the design was unsafe, but because the application was incomplete. The NRC cited insufficient safety analysis documentation. Oklo has been in extensive pre-application dialogue with the NRC since and submitted a Principal Design Criteria (PDC) Topical Report that the NRC accepted for review under an accelerated timeline in September 2025. The NRC has signaled its commitment to modernizing licensing for advanced reactors, and the regulatory environment under the current administration has been constructive for nuclear development.

But here's the hard truth: Oklo cannot generate a single watt of commercial power until the NRC approves its combined license application. That process — even on an accelerated track — likely takes 2-3 more years from application submission. A realistic timeline for the first Aurora Powerhouse generating commercial power is 2028-2030. Every signed agreement, every partnership, every GW of pipeline is contingent on that license arriving and the construction going as planned. Regulatory risk is the tail risk that can zero the stock — it's a low probability but non-trivial event.
