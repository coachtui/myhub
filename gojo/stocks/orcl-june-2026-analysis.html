What the Business Actually Looks Like

Oracle is no longer just a database company that happened to build a cloud. By FY2026, it has reorganized itself around three distinct growth engines operating at the same time:

1. Oracle Cloud Infrastructure (OCI) — Oracle’s hyperscale cloud platform, competing with AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud but with a differentiated architecture. OCI’s RDMA networking and GPU cluster density made it the preferred platform for AI training workloads that require extreme inter-node bandwidth. While it remains smaller than the three giants, it has been gaining share specifically in the AI training segment where architectural differentiation matters most.

2. The Stargate Joint Venture — Oracle is one of three founding partners (alongside OpenAI and SoftBank) in Stargate, the largest AI infrastructure project ever announced. Initial capacity is anchored at Abilene, Texas, with multi-site expansion planned across the U.S. The market read Stargate’s announcement as a structural endorsement of OCI’s capabilities. The financial impact shows up in Oracle’s RPO (remaining performance obligations) — the contracted-but-not-yet-recognized revenue backlog that has grown to levels that imply years of visibility.

3. Cloud Applications + Database — Fusion ERP, HCM, and NetSuite continue to compound at double-digit rates. Oracle also struck MultiCloud partnerships with AWS, Azure, and Google, meaning Oracle Database runs natively inside competing hyperscaler environments — turning what was a zero-sum fight into a growth engine. The Cerner healthcare acquisition, which had a painful integration period, is now contributing constructively.

Why the Stock Fell 57% While the Business Grew

The disconnect has three layers:

Layer 1 — It was priced for perfection. At $345, Oracle was trading at a multiple that priced in both perfect execution on Stargate and immediate FCF conversion. When Q2 FY2026 (December 2025) showed mixed results and guidance that didn’t accelerate as aggressively as bulls hoped, the multiple compressed fast. The January 2026 selloff took another 15%+ off on top of that.

Layer 2 — DeepSeek and AI efficiency concerns. When DeepSeek demonstrated that frontier AI models could be built with a fraction of the assumed compute, the market repriced the entire AI infrastructure buildout narrative. Companies like Oracle — which had been valued on the assumption that AI demand would grow exponentially and indefinitely — got hit disproportionately. The thesis didn’t break; it just got questioned.

Layer 3 — Capex ate free cash flow. Oracle has been aggressively issuing debt and spending to build data center capacity for Stargate and OCI expansion. FCF compressed as that spending accelerated. The market hates paying growth multiples for a company burning cash on construction. Q4 FY2026 beat on revenue and EPS, then fell 10% anyway because capex guidance and the conversion timeline didn’t satisfy investors.

The AI Restructuring Story Nobody Talks About

Oracle cut 21,000 employees in fiscal 2026 — roughly 13% of its entire workforce. Part of this is AI automation replacing roles inside Oracle’s own operations. This is a company that is simultaneously selling AI infrastructure to the world and using AI to restructure its own cost base. When the capex cycle matures and FCF recovers, the leaner headcount structure sets up margin expansion that isn’t fully priced in.
